## LINKEDIN

My son asked me three questions about AI in healthcare one evening.

I answered the first two confidently.

The third one stopped me completely.

---

**Question 1:** "Nowadays, people ask AI their health questions directly. Won't doctors lose their jobs?"

I gave the answer I've given at medical conferences: AI gives information. Doctors give accountability, trust, and human judgment. People will always want a doctor when something truly matters.

He nodded. I thought we were done.

---

**Question 2:** "But millions of people are asking AI health questions every minute. If a human has to check each one — how? There aren't enough doctors."

He had found the real problem faster than most governance committees do.

I explained the architecture: a medical AI handles the conversation, a supervising AI filters the outputs for safety and clinical accuracy, and only what the supervising AI flags ever reaches a human clinician. The rest passes through without any human eyes ever seeing it.

"So," he said, "the supervising AI decides what doctors see — and what they never see."

"Yes," I said. "Essentially."

---

**Question 3 — the one I couldn't answer:**

"Then who will supervise the supervising AI?"

Because if the supervising AI fails to flag something dangerous — if it misses something — that advice never reaches a human. No doctor sees it. No one catches it. The patient acts on it alone.

That is the real danger. Not wrong advice. Wrong advice, and the safety net failing quietly, with no one watching.

---

My son asked this casually, at the end of an ordinary evening, without knowing he had landed on one of the oldest unsolved problems in governance — the same one Juvenal asked two thousand years ago: *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?* Who guards the guardians?

The question is not new. But the stakes — healthcare, scale, speed — have never been higher.

## MEDIUM

**Title:** My Son Asked Three Questions About AI in Healthcare. The Third One I Couldn't Answer.

**Subtitle:** A child's logic led to one of the oldest governance problems in the world — and one of the most urgent in digital health.

---

It began the way many of our best conversations do — not at a scheduled time, not with any plan, but with my son coming to sit beside me while I was working.

He looked at my screen for a moment. I am a doctor. He knows this. What he saw — medical literature, AI governance papers, health informatics research — was the work of someone watching a field change faster than any generation before it has had to watch medicine change.

His first question arrived quietly.

**"Nowadays, people ask AI their health questions directly. Won't doctors lose their jobs?"**

I gave the answer I have given at medical conferences: AI gives information. Doctors give accountability, experience, and trust. When a decision truly matters — when a person is frightened, when something is at stake — they still want a human.

He nodded. I thought the conversation was settled.

**Then came the second question.**

"Millions of people are asking AI health questions every minute. If a human has to check each one — how? There aren't enough doctors."

He had found the real problem faster than most governance discussions do.

I explained the architecture that makes it manageable at scale. A medical AI handles the conversation. A supervising AI — a second, separate system — filters the outputs for safety violations and clinical inconsistencies. Only what the supervising AI flags ever reaches a human clinician. The rest passes through without any human ever seeing it.

"So," he said, "the supervising AI decides what doctors see — and what they never see."

"Yes," I said. "Essentially."

**Then the third question. The one I couldn't answer.**

*"Then who will supervise the supervising AI?"*

Because if the supervising AI fails to flag something dangerous — if it has a blind spot it was never tested for — that advice never reaches a human. No doctor sees it. No correction is made. The person who asked acts on it alone.

That is the real danger. Not wrong advice. Wrong advice, and the safety net failing quietly, with no one watching.

---

My son asked this casually, at the end of an ordinary evening, without knowing he had landed on one of the oldest unsolved problems in governance. The Roman poet Juvenal asked it two thousand years ago: *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?* — Who guards the guardians?

Every governance system humanity has ever designed has faced this same challenge. Regulators need regulators. Auditors require auditing. At some point, the chain of supervisors must stop. And what it stops at had better be trustworthy — because everything above it depends on it.

Artificial intelligence has not invented this problem. What it has done is bring it into a context where the systems are fast, the scale is enormous, the stakes include human health — and the governance structures are still catching up.

---

The answer is not to add another layer of AI on top. Trust is not built by adding layers. It is built through transparency, accountability, and correction mechanisms — and through the willingness to ask uncomfortable questions early, rather than waiting until the consequences have become visible.

A child asked that question at the end of an ordinary evening. He asked it because the logic led him there — and because he had not yet learned to stop following a question when it becomes inconvenient.

That may be the most valuable thing a person can bring to this conversation.

## SUBSTACK

**Subject line:** The question my son asked that I couldn't answer

---

Dear reader,

One evening a few weeks ago, my son came to sit beside me while I was working.

He looked at my screen for a moment — medical literature, AI governance papers, health informatics research — and asked his first question.

*"Nowadays, people ask AI their health questions directly. Won't doctors lose their jobs?"*

I answered confidently. I have answered this question at conferences. AI gives information. Doctors give accountability and trust. People still want a human when something truly matters.

He nodded. Then came the second question.

*"But millions of people ask AI health questions every minute. If a human needs to check each one — how? There aren't enough doctors."*

This one pushed me further. I explained the architecture: a medical AI handles conversations, a supervising AI filters outputs for safety and clinical accuracy, and only what gets flagged ever reaches a human clinician. The rest passes through unseen.

*"So the supervising AI decides what doctors see — and what they never see."*

Yes, I told him. Essentially.

Then came the third question. The one I had no answer for.

*"Then who will supervise the supervising AI?"*

Because if the supervising AI misses something dangerous — fails to flag it, has a blind spot it was never tested for — that advice never reaches any human. The patient acts on it alone. Not because anyone lied. Because the safety net failed quietly, and no one was watching the safety net.

---

My son asked this at the end of an ordinary evening. He asked it because the logic led him there — and because he had not yet learned to stop following a question when it becomes uncomfortable.

That question is two thousand years old. Juvenal asked it in Rome: *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?* Who guards the guardians?

AI has not invented this problem. It has brought it into a context where the scale is vast, the speed is extraordinary, the stakes include human health — and the governance structures are still catching up.
