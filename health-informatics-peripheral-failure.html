Why Centralised Health Information Systems Fail in Peripheral Care Settings

A nurse asked me a question I could not answer. She had no research papers. She had twenty years of experience entering data into a system that never told her why — and never gave anything back.

Dr. T. Jeevaraj MBBS · MCGP · MSc Biomedical Informatics · MD Trainee, Health Informatics · PGIM, University of Colombo
· 21 June 2026 · Health Informatics · 1,650 words
“

I don't know why they are collecting this much data. I don't know what the benefit is. Most of the time I am confused. Our junior officers are struggling. We enter data manually, then calculate, then enter again into the digital system — but what is the output? Nobody knows.

A peripheral healthcare worker, Sri Lanka (details generalised to protect identity)

The central office and the peripheral voice — two realities of the same health information system. Illustration: Dr. T. Jeevaraj, 2026.

I was sitting at my desk in a central health department office when I made the call. Peripheral facilities had been submitting delayed and incomplete data to the central repository, and part of my responsibility was to follow up. I telephoned one of the nurses responsible for data entry at a divisional facility in the district.

She answered. She did not apologise for the delay. She did not offer a technical explanation. Instead, after a brief pause, she described her situation — quietly, carefully, and with the kind of precision that comes not from training but from years of accumulated frustration that has finally found a sympathetic ear.

"I don't know why they are collecting this much data," she said. "I don't know what the benefit is. Most of the time I am confused about what to do with this. Our junior officers are facing a lot of difficulty. Once we enter data in the manual system, we calculate it, then we enter again into the digital system. But what is the output? Why are we doing this much struggle? Nobody knows."

I listened without interrupting her. Because what she had just described was not a complaint to be logged and passed up the chain. It was a clinical diagnosis of the system itself. She had identified, without a single citation or academic framework, the core structural failure of centralised health information systems: they extract data from the periphery without returning value to the people who generate that data. They impose burden without offering benefit. They are designed for the centre, at the centre, by the centre.

I understood what she was describing because I had lived on the other side of that telephone for twenty years. Her words deserved to be the starting point of a serious conversation about system design — not a performance indicator report.

Twenty Years in the Periphery

From 2007 to 2026, I worked almost entirely in peripheral care settings in Trincomalee district: as a medical officer in the inward units and MICU of a general hospital, as a general practitioner in two primary medical care units, as the Medical Officer in Charge of a divisional hospital, and as a public health officer at the Regional Director of Health Services office. Each of these settings had its own relationship with data — and its own form of quiet resistance to the systems imposed from above.

At the GP clinic in Kappalthurai, I saw forty or more patients in a day. Between consultations, I was expected to maintain manual registers, complete HMIS forms, update NCD tracking cards, enter data into web portals on a connection that dropped without warning, and compile statistics for monthly reports that were due whether or not we had the time — or the electricity — to compile them accurately. The data went up. The information never came down.

No dashboard. No feedback. No indication of whether the figures we submitted bore any relationship to the patterns the district or ministry understood about our population. The data was collected. What happened to it next was invisible from where we stood.

Key Observation
Data flows upward. Information never flows down.

Peripheral healthcare workers generate the raw material of every national health information system. They do this at considerable personal cost — time taken from patient care, mental bandwidth diverted from clinical judgement, administrative burden accumulated on top of an already stretched workload. In return, most peripheral facilities receive no feedback from the system they feed. They do not see district comparisons. They do not see trend analyses. They do not know whether their data changed a policy, triggered a resource reallocation, or disappeared into a central database without consequence. The system is extractive in effect.

What the Centre Assumes the Periphery Has

Health information systems are typically designed by technical teams working at the central or regional level — ministry officials, WHO advisors, software engineers, health informatics consultants. These teams are competent. They are often well-intentioned. But they design within the logic of their own environment: offices with reliable internet connections, dedicated data entry staff, standardised workflows, and access to the output dashboards that justify the work of data collection.

The peripheral reality is structurally different. Not marginally different — structurally different.

The result of these mismatched assumptions is predictable. Peripheral healthcare workers do not refuse to enter data. They adapt. They complete forms as best they can, under the conditions they have. They batch entries. They estimate. They enter what the form asks rather than what the clinical encounter actually contained. The data arrives at the centre looking complete. The centre reads it as complete. The system appears to function.

What the central dashboard does not show is the quality of the data behind it, the human cost of producing it, or the clinical information that was lost in the translation from encounter to field to row in a database.

The Double Entry Problem and the Invisible Purpose

The nurse described a phenomenon that is almost universal in resource-limited health systems: double entry. Data is first recorded manually — in the register, the outpatient book, the NCD card, the maternal health record. At the end of the day, the week, or the month, someone — usually the same clinical staff member who saw the patients — must extract figures from those manual records, calculate aggregate totals, and enter them again into the digital system.

This process was not designed to create this burden. It emerged from a sequence of decisions made without adequate peripheral input. The digital system was added to a health facility that already had established manual workflows. No one assessed whether the existing manual system would be replaced or supplemented. No one asked the peripheral nurse whether the digital form's fields corresponded to what she was already recording on paper. No one built an interface that could import manual data directly. The result was a doubled workload created by digitisation rather than reduced by it.

Once we enter data in the manual system, we calculate it, then we enter again into the digital system. But what is the output? Why are we doing this much struggle?

— A peripheral healthcare worker, Sri Lanka (paraphrased with permission; identifying details removed)

The question "what is the output?" is not rhetorical. It reflects a genuine information vacuum. The nurse who enters the data does not know what it produces. She has never been shown a district-level analysis that used her facility's figures. She has never received a report indicating that the NCD prevalence data she submitted contributed to a resource planning decision. She has never seen the dashboard her data feeds. She knows the data goes somewhere. She does not know what it becomes when it gets there. In the absence of visible purpose, the work feels purposeless — because from where she stands, it is.

From Practice
The "end-of-month rush" is not a compliance problem — it is a system design problem

In peripheral settings, it is common for thirty days of patient data to be entered in the final two days before the monthly reporting deadline. This is often misread at the centre as a discipline or motivation problem. It is not. It reflects the impossibility of real-time data entry in a single-doctor facility with continuous patient flow and no dedicated data entry staff. The double-entry burden — manual registration followed by digital submission — makes concurrent entry practically unachievable. The workaround is retrospective batch entry. The data arrives on time. The data quality is compromised. The centre sees completion. The peripheral worker is exhausted.

The Voice That Was Never Invited

There is a governance failure embedded in this situation that precedes and produces all the technical failures. The nurse who described the problem to me had not been asked. Not once, in the years she had been entering data into the system, had anyone from the district or ministry office invited her perspective on whether the system worked, what the barriers were, what information she needed in return, or what a better system would look like. She had never sat in a design meeting. She had never reviewed a form before it was finalised. She had never been asked to test a digital interface before it was deployed to her facility.

This is not unusual. It is the default. Health information systems are typically designed through a process that involves ministry planning offices, technical consultants, international advisors, and software development teams. Site visits to peripheral facilities are sometimes conducted — usually briefly, usually by senior officials who are received differently than the staff experience their daily conditions, and usually to assess infrastructure rather than to elicit design input from frontline workers.

The consequence is a system designed around the information needs of the centre rather than the information capacity of the periphery. The centre knows what it wants to receive. It builds a system to receive it. The periphery is the source — and the periphery's constraints, knowledge, and needs are treated as implementation challenges rather than design inputs.

What Moving to the Centre Revealed

I now work at the central level. I sit in offices with reliable internet connections. I have access to the dashboards the nurse has never seen. I attend meetings where district-level data is analysed, where graphs are drawn, where policy discussions reference statistics that peripheral healthcare workers spent exhausting hours producing.

What this transition has shown me is not incompetence at the centre. The people working in central health information offices are capable, committed, and genuinely trying to build functional systems. What I have observed is a structural absence: peripheral voices are not in the room when systems are designed. The people who understand what it costs to generate the data — what it takes, what it disrupts, what it competes with — are not present when decisions are made about what data to collect, in what format, on what timeline, through what interface.

The result is systems that are technically functional and humanly unworkable. Systems that produce dashboards at the centre and exhaustion at the periphery. Systems that measure completeness rather than quality. Systems that are optimised for reporting rather than for the clinical and administrative realities of the people who operate them.

What Better Design Requires

The nurse's question — what is the output? — is both a diagnosis and a design specification. A health information system that peripheral healthcare workers would trust, use accurately, and engage with voluntarily would need to answer that question visibly and concretely.

This requires more than user interface improvements. It requires a fundamental reorientation of how health information systems are designed and governed.

✦ Design Principles
What peripheral-first health information system design requires
  • Structural inclusion of peripheral workers in design processes. Not consultations after decisions are made — participation from the moment system requirements are being defined. The nurse who enters the data should review the form before it is finalised.
  • Visible return on data entry. Every peripheral facility that submits data should receive, in return, a simple local analysis of that data — facility-level trends, comparison against district averages, early warning flags. The data must visibly do something for the people who generate it.
  • Elimination of double entry. Digital systems should be designed to replace manual registers, not supplement them. Where parallel systems must temporarily coexist, the digital form must map directly to the manual record — not require separate calculation and re-entry.
  • Offline-first infrastructure. Forms must be completable without an internet connection, with synchronisation occurring when connectivity is available. Designing for connectivity that does not reliably exist at peripheral level is designing for failure.
  • Minimum viable data sets. Every data field should be justified by its direct use. Fields that feed analyses the peripheral worker will never see, for purposes the peripheral worker was never told about, should be removed or made optional.

These are not novel recommendations. The implementation science literature has extensively documented what happens when health information systems are introduced without adequate sociotechnical alignment — a term describing the fit between a technology and the social, organisational, and workflow conditions of its users. Heeks (2006), in a widely cited analysis of health information system failures in developing countries, identified the gap between the "world as imagined" by designers and the "world as is" in practice as the primary predictor of implementation failure.1 The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025 explicitly recognises the need for digital health systems to be "designed with and for the people who use them," naming participatory design as a prerequisite for sustainable adoption.2 The gap between this principle and current practice in peripheral health information system deployment remains substantial.

Implications for Healthcare AI

This failure does not remain confined to health information systems. It is already being reproduced in healthcare artificial intelligence. AI systems built to support clinical decision-making, disease surveillance, or resource allocation inherit their training data from the same health information infrastructure described above — data generated under the same constraints, compressed into the same inadequate fields, submitted on the same impossible timelines, by the same peripheral healthcare workers who were never asked what was missing.

It is reasonable to expect that an AI trained on peripheral health data will carry the structural biases of peripheral data collection: underreported conditions that required more time to record than was available, demographic gaps where patients never reached a registered facility, disease patterns compressed into the categories the form allowed rather than the clinical reality that existed. The AI cannot see what was omitted. It can only learn from what was entered.

More fundamentally, healthcare AI is now being designed through the same logic as the health information systems that preceded it — by technical teams working at the centre, for clinical problems whose peripheral complexity they have not directly experienced. The peripheral clinician who will use the AI tool is rarely in the room where that tool is designed. The feedback loop that would allow AI to return visible value to peripheral healthcare workers — to answer the nurse's question, what is the output? — is rarely built in.

The design problem that this article describes is not a legacy problem waiting to be fixed by the next generation of technology. It is the default logic of how health technology is built. Without a deliberate change in that logic, artificial intelligence in healthcare will inherit and amplify the same failure it was supposed to correct.

The argument developed here does not end with health information systems. It extends to clinical workflow design, to healthcare AI, and to the governance frameworks that determine who has the authority to shape tools that affect patients and healthcare workers. The question the nurse asked on the telephone — what is the output? — is not only a question about health data systems. It is a question about who gets to ask design questions, and who has been excluded from asking them long enough that they no longer expect to be invited.

She was not complaining. She was diagnosing. And the diagnosis was correct.
